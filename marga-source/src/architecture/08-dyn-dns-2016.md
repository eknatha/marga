# Dyn DNS attack, October 2016
company: Dyn
date: 21 October 2016
modules: networking, cloud, sre
calc: redundancy, error-budget
source: Dyn analysis summary | https://dyn.com/blog/dyn-analysis-summary-of-friday-october-21-attack/
summary: A botnet of everyday devices flooded one DNS provider, and sites with a single DNS provider disappeared for part of the world.

## Before you read on
If your only DNS provider were unreachable, how many minutes would it take before your users noticed, and what could you do in advance to avoid depending on one provider?

## The system
Dyn operated a managed DNS service used by many large internet companies. Authoritative DNS answers are served from many locations by anycast. DNS resolvers cache answers for the time to live (TTL) of each record and, when they cannot get an answer, they retry and ask other servers of the same provider.

## Timeline as reported
- Around 11:10 UTC on Friday 21 October 2016, a DDoS attack began against Dyn's DNS service. Users on the US East Coast saw sites such as Twitter and Netflix fail to load.
- Dyn mitigated the first attack after a couple of hours.
- A second wave began in the afternoon and affected a wider area. A third wave followed later in the day, which Dyn also mitigated.
- The traffic came from a large botnet, later linked to Mirai, made of compromised internet-connected devices such as cameras and recorders.
- Resolvers that could not get an answer retried, which added load to the system under attack.

## Root cause
The attack was larger than the capacity of the service in the affected locations, and was made of legitimate-looking DNS queries from many devices, so it was hard to filter. Customers had a single provider, so there was no alternative path for resolvers.

## What changed
- Dyn improved its mitigation and capacity. Many customers added a second DNS provider and reviewed TTL values.
- The incident increased attention on the security of consumer devices.

## Trade-offs to weigh
- **One provider against two.** Two providers protect against provider failure, but you must keep the zones in sync, and some provider-specific features, such as traffic steering, may not work across both.
- **Long TTL against short TTL.** A longer TTL lets resolvers keep answering during an outage, while a shorter one lets you change records quickly.
- **Anycast and scale.** Spreading across locations raises capacity, but attacks scale too.
